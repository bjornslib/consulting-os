# Board Memos & Decisions

A board memo is a decision instrument that must work without you in the room. Directors are time-poor, personally accountable, and professionally sceptical — the memo respects all three: the ask on page one, the case against presented honestly, and a decision worded precisely enough to minute.

## Method

**1. Lead with the ask.** First paragraph: what decision is requested, in exact words ("The Board is asked to approve $X to do Y over Z period, subject to conditions A and B"). Approvals, funding, conditions, and delegations are all explicit — vague asks get deferred, and deferral is the default outcome of an unclear paper.

**2. Structure for a director's reading.**
- **The ask** (as above) and why now — what makes this decision due at this meeting.
- **Context in one page** — only what is needed to judge this decision; the board has history, so do not re-teach it.
- **The case** — the recommendation's logic, key evidence, and the economics (headline NPV/payback/peak funding, scenarios summarised).
- **The case against** — the strongest objections and downside scenario, steel-manned. Directors will find weaknesses; a paper that found them first earns trust, and a paper that hid them spends it.
- **Risks and mitigations** — top 5, in dollars and time, with treatments and owners (from the risk register, not invented for the paper).
- **Alternatives considered** — the runner-up option and why it lost; "do nothing" with its honest trajectory.
- **Governance details** — approvals sought, delegation limits, conditions, reporting-back commitments, and any conflicts of interest declared.

**3. Word the resolution for the minutes.** Draft the exact resolution text: unambiguous scope, amount, authority, conditions, and review point. If the resolution cannot be written crisply, the ask is not yet clear — go back.

**4. Calibrate length and anticipate the room.** 3–6 pages of argument; models and detail in appendices. Use the stakeholder map to anticipate the two or three questions particular directors will ask, and answer them in the body or a pre-empting appendix. Where the chair's convention differs (some boards want one page, some want fifteen), the convention wins — ask if unknown.

**5. Close the loop.** Every memo commits to a report-back: when the board will hear how the decision performed against the case made. This discipline is what keeps future memos honest.
