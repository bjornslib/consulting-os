# Customer Segmentation

A segmentation is a decision tool, not a demographic poster. Segments must differ in what they need or how they buy, be identifiable in the real world, and be reachable through distinct actions — otherwise they are trivia.

## Method

**1. Choose the segmentation basis by the decision it serves.**
- **Needs/jobs-based** — for proposition and product decisions (the most strategically powerful, hardest to observe).
- **Behavioural** — usage, spend, churn, channel; for retention, pricing, and lifecycle decisions.
- **Firmographic/demographic** — for targeting and sales-coverage decisions (observable but shallow; use as the *identification layer* for a needs-based scheme, not as the scheme itself).

**2. Build candidate segments.** With data: cluster on behaviour/needs variables, then profile with observable traits. Without data: hypothesise segments from interviews and the value chain, and mark the scheme as hypothesis-grade until validated.

**3. Test the scheme against the four criteria:**
- **Distinct** — segments differ materially in needs or economics.
- **Identifiable** — you can tell which segment a real customer belongs to using observable markers.
- **Substantial** — each segment is worth serving (size × margin).
- **Actionable** — the client can actually vary product, price, channel, or message by segment.

Fail any criterion → redesign, don't decorate.

**4. Economically weight the segments.** Revenue, margin, growth, acquisition cost, and retention per segment. The point is to find where value concentrates — this connects directly to profit-pool work.

**5. Prioritise and name.** 3–7 segments; memorable names that describe the need or behaviour, not just size ("Compliance-driven laggards", not "Segment C").
