# Pricing Strategy

Price is the highest-leverage profit variable and the most under-managed. This skill treats pricing as three linked decisions: the **metric** (what you charge for), the **architecture** (how offers are structured), and the **level** (the number) — in that order, because most pricing failures are metric or architecture failures wearing a level costume.

## Method

**1. Establish the value baseline.** For each key segment: the economic value the offer creates (cost saved, revenue enabled, risk reduced), the next-best alternative and its price, and observable willingness-to-pay signals (win/loss at price points, discount patterns, upgrade behaviour). Cost sets the floor; value and the alternative set the ceiling; strategy chooses the point between.

**2. Choose the price metric.** The metric should scale with the value received, be measurable and predictable for the buyer, and be hard to game. For AI and software offers this is often the crux: per seat vs per usage vs per outcome vs platform fee — each shifts risk between buyer and seller and shapes adoption. State the trade-off explicitly.

**3. Design the architecture.** Tiers/packages mapped to segments from segmentation work: each tier anchored to a segment's needs, with deliberate fencing (features, limits, service levels) so segments self-select. Add: the anchor tier, the upgrade path, and what is deliberately left out of lower tiers.

**4. Set levels.** Combine value baseline, competitive reference prices, and price-sensitivity evidence. Model the volume–margin trade-off at candidate levels rather than asserting elasticity. Where evidence is thin, design a test (regional, cohort, or new-customer pilot) before a global move.

**5. Govern discounting.** Define the list-to-realised waterfall, discount authority levels, and what discounts buy (term, volume, reference) — unmanaged discounting quietly repeals the strategy.

**Legal line**: never coordinate prices with competitors or advise mechanisms whose purpose is collusion. Competitive reference pricing from public information is fine; agreement is not.
