# Business Cases & Models

A business case exists to let a decision-maker say yes or no with clear eyes. That requires a driver-based model (not a spreadsheet of grown percentages), honest scenarios, and the case *against* presented as seriously as the case for.

## Method

**1. Structure the model around drivers.** Revenue = volume drivers × price (from pricing work); costs split into fixed/variable and one-off/recurring; investment phased over time. Every line traces to an operational driver someone can influence and measure — "revenue grows 15%" is an output, never an input.

**2. Source every input.** Inputs come from the fact base (graded) or the assumption register (flagged). The model inherits the honesty of its inputs; an input tab with sources and grades is non-negotiable.

**3. Build three genuine scenarios.** Base, downside, upside — constructed from coherent driver stories ("adoption at half the pace, competitor responds on price"), not ±20% on everything. The downside must be a scenario the sponsor believes could happen. Add break-even: what adoption/price/cost makes NPV zero.

**4. Compute the decision metrics.** NPV at the client's hurdle rate, payback period, peak funding requirement, and IRR if the client uses it. State the discount rate and why. Payback and peak cash matter to operators even when NPV headlines the page.

**5. Run sensitivity honestly.** Tornado on the top drivers; identify the 2–3 inputs that swing the answer. Those inputs get the assumption-audit treatment and, ideally, a de-risking action (pilot, contract structure, staged commitment).

**6. Present the case against.** One section, written as a competent sceptic would: the strongest reasons not to proceed and what evidence would validate them. A case that cannot state its own best objection is advocacy, not analysis.

Before drafting the final deliverable, load `references/deliverable.md` for the output template and quality bar.
